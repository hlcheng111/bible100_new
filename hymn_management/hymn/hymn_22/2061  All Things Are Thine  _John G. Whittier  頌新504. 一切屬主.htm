1 All things are Yours; nothing of ours,
we bring to You, Lord of all powers;
and hence with grateful hearts today
Your own before Your feet we lay.

2 Your will was in the builders' thought;
Your hand unseen amidst us wrought;
through mortal motive, scheme and plan,
Your wise eternal purpose ran.

3 In weakness and in want we call
on You, for whom the heavens are small;
Your glory is Your children's good,
Your joy, Your tender fatherhood.

4 O Lord, be pleased these walls to bless;
fill with Your love their emptiness,
and let their door become a way
to lead us to eternal day.

Whittier, John Greenleaf,

the American Quaker poet, was born at Haverhill, Massachusetts, Dec. 17, 1807. He began life as a farm-boy and shoemaker, and subsequently became a successful journalist, editor and poet. In 1828 he became editor of the American Manufacturer (Boston), in 1830 of the New England Review, and an 1836 (on becoming Secretary to the American Anti-Slavery Society) of the Pennsylvania Freeman. He was also for some time, beginning with 1847, the corresponding editor of the National Era. In 1840 he removed to Amesbury, Massachusetts, where most of his later works have been written. At the present time [1890] he lives alternately at Amesbury and Boston. His first poetical piece was printed in the Newburyport Free Press in 1824. Since then his publications have been numerous, including:—
Voices of Freedom, 1833; Songs of Labour, and other Poems, 1850; Ballads and other Poems, London, 1844; The Panorama, and other Poems, 1856; In War Time, 1863; Occasional Poems, 1865; Poetical Works, 1869; Complete Poetical Works, 1876; The Bay of the Seven Islands, and other Poems, 1883, &c.

All things are thine: no gift have we

All things are thine: no gift have we. John Greenleaf Whittier* (1807-1892). Written in 1872 for the opening of Plymouth Church, St Paul, Minnesota, presumably on request. It had five stanzas, with a graceful reference in stanza 3 to the geographical location: No lack Thy perfect fullness knew; For human needs and longings grew This house of prayer, this home of rest, In the fair garden of the West. This local reference has led to the omission of this stanza in many hymnals. Without it, the hymn is a very appropriate celebration for a new church, especially in a new building

“ALL THINGS ARE THINE”

“…For all things come of Thee, and of Thine own have we given Thee” (1 Chron. 29.15)

     INTRO.: 

A song which reminds us that everything we have comes from the Lord and whatever we give to Him is something that He has already given us is “All Things Are Thine.”  The text was written by John Greenleaf Whittier (1807-1892).  A Quaker poet, he said that he could not write hymns, although many portions of his poems have been adapted as hymns, such as “Dear Lord and Father of Mankind,” “Immortal Love, Forever Full,” “We May Not Climb the Heavenly Steeps,” and “O Brother Man,” which have appeared in many of our books, along with some forth others.  His only poem actually intended to be a hymn was “All Things Are Thine,” which was produced in 1872 for the opening of the Plymouth Church in Minneapolis, MN (sometimes the date 1873 is given; this may be when it first appeared publicly in print, or it could just be a mistake).  It was first published in his 1876 Complete Poetical Works, and first published as a hymn in W. Garrett Horder’s Congregational Hymns of 1884.  In many denominational hymnals, only the first stanza is used as “service music” for dedication or offering.

Many tunes have been used with the hymn.   The one I prefer (O Jesu Christe, Wahres Licht) was taken from the 1676 Andachtige Haus-Kirche Gesangbuch of Nurnberg, where it was used with Johann Heerman’s “O Christ, Our Light, O Radiance True.”  It was in quadruple meter.  The tune was adapted into its present triple-meter form by Johannes Christoph Andreas Zahn (1817-1895).  He was a German theologian and musicologist best known for his opus Die Melodien der deutschen evangelischen Kirchenlieder, a critical anthology of almost 9000 hymn melodies developed and used in German Lutheran churches.  So far as I know, the song has appeared in no songbooks published by members of the Lord’s church during the twentieth century for use in churches of Christ.

However, in my collection of hymnbooks from various other sources, it does appear in one form or another in the following:  Hymnal: Church of the Brethren (Brethren Publishing House, 1925), edited by John S. Flory, with an 1815 tune (Gardiner or Germany) attributed to William Gardiner (in our books used with “Where Cross the Crowded Ways of Life”); The New Hymnal for American Youth (D. Appleton Century Co., 1930), edited by H. Augustine Smith, with an 1899 tune (Ecclesfield) by Alfred S. Scott-Gatty;   Pilgrim Hymnal (Pilgrim Press, 1931), edited by James W. Lenhart, with a 1648 tune (Herr Jesu Christ or Pensum Sacrum) arranged by Johann S. Bach (in our books used with “Lord Jesus Christ, Be Present Now”);  The Broadman Hymnal (Broadman Press, 1940), edited by B. B. McKinney (with Herr Jesu Christ);  The Hymnal of the Evangelical United Brethren Church (The Board of Publication of the Evangelical United Brethren Church, 1957), ed ited by J. Balmer Showers, twice, with an 1830 tune (Uxbridge) by Lowell Mason (in our books used with “God, In the Gospel of His Son) and with Herr Jesu Christ;  The Methodist Hymnal (The Methodist Publishing House, 1964), edited by Carlton Young (with Herr Jesu Christ);   Christian Praise (Broadman Press, 1964), no editor named (with Gardiner/Germany);  The Mennonite Hymnal (Herald Press/Faith and Life Press, 1969), edited by Vernon H. Neufeld;  Hymns for the Living Church (Hope Publishing Company, 1974), edited by Donald P. Hustad (with Herr Jesu Christ); and Trinity Hymnal Revised (Great Commissions Publications, 1990), edited by Lawrence C. Roff (with Herr Jesu Christ).

The song emphasizes the fact that all things belong to the Lord and should be used to His glory.

 

I. Stanza 1 teaches that all things come from God

“All things are Thine; no gift have we,

Lord of all gifts, to offer Thee.

And hence with grateful hearts today,

Thine own before Thy feet we lay.”

  1. Because He is the Creator, all things belong to God: Ps. 50.12
  2. We are to offer ourselves as living sacrifices to the Lord: Rom. 12.1
  3. However, whatever we give to Him in so doing we are simply returning to Him who gave it as good stewards: Matt. 25.14-30

 

II. Stanza 2 teaches that God’s will should be done in everything

“Thy will was in the builders’ thought;

Thy hand unseen amidst us wrought.

Through mortal motive, scheme, and plan,

Thy wise eternal purpose ran.”

  1. Those who build, whether it be physically or spiritually, will do so in vain unless the Lord builds with them: Ps. 127.1
  2. Yet, when people build in harmony with God’s will, then His hand works amidst them because He has built all things: Heb. 3.4
  3. Therefore, we need to make sure that all our motives, schemes, and plans are according to His wise eternal purpose: Eph. 3.10-11

 

III. Stanza 3 teaches us that God will supply our needs

“No lack Thy perfect fullness knew,

For human needs and longings grew

This house of prayer, this home of rest,

In the fair garden of the West.”

  1. Those who truly serve God will know no lack because God will supply their needs: Phil. 4.19
  2. We understand that a church meeting place does not fall into the same category as the temple in the Old Testament, but if it is being erected with funds donated for the work of the church, then it has grown out of human needs and longings to assemble according to Biblical teaching: Heb. 10.25
  3. And in this way, it is to be used only for that which the Lord has authorized as a place of prayer: Matt. 21.13 (at the time Whittier wrote this, Minnesota would have been considered part of the frontier of “the West.” Because of this geographical identification, most books that use more than the first stanza usually omit this one.)

 

IV. Stanza 4 teaches us that we should call on God for what we need

“In weakness and in want we call

On Thee for whom the heavens are small;

Thy glory is Thy children’s good,

Thy joy Thy tender fatherhood.”

  1. Even though no building can contain the great God, whenever His people pay to Him, He has promised to hear: 1 Ki. 8.27-30
  2. Today, Christians are His children and they can expect Him to respond to their prayers by giving what is good: Matt. 7.7-12
  3. Therefore, they look to Him as the Father from whom every good and perfect gift comes: Matt. 6.9, Jas. 1.17

 

V. Stanza 5 teaches that we should ask God blessings on our endeavors

“O Father, deign these walls to bless;

Fill with Thy love their emptiness.

And let their door a gateway be

To lead us from ourselves to Thee.”

  1. By metonymy, asking God “these walls to bless” is in essence asking Him to bless how they are used and what goes on within them in worship and Bible study: Jas. 1.25
  2. Of course, we should seek to be filled with God’s love at all times, but it is not inappropriate to pray that when we assemble together that God’s love fill us in a special way: Eph. 3.19
  3. In this way, what we do in our places of assembly serves as a gateway to lead us from ourselves closer to God: Jas. 4.8

 

CONCL.: 

Some people have objected to Whittier’s hymns in general because, they say, as a Quaker he had little sense of sin and thus of man’s great need of grace.  However, whatever Whittier’s personal beliefs may have been, I challenge anyone to read his “Dear Lord and Father of Mankind” and reach that conclusion.  Not every hymn has to be a full repetition of the scheme of redemption!  Others might question a hymn which asks God to bless a physical structure, but if we understand that such a structure is being used to accomplish God’s purposes, it may be considered an appropriate petition when people enter a house of worship to praise God.  And the song certainly reminds us that our attitude toward everything that we have in this life should be that of telling the Lord, “All Things Are Thine.”
