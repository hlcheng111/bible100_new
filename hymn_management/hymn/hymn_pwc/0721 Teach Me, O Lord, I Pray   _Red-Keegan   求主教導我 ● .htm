Gilbert Kearnie Keegan

Born: January 31, 1907, Bunkie, Louisiana.
Died: September 13, 1960, St. Louis, Missouri. He died just after boarding a plane to Hawaii.
Buried: Woodlawn Memorial Park and Mausoleum, Nashville, Tennessee.

Keegan was a graduate of Northwestern State College, Natchitoches, Louisiana (BA 1927), and Southwestern Baptist Theological Seminary, Fort Worth, Texas (ThM 1933). He pastored at the First Baptist Church, Natchitoches, Louisiana (1932-35); Emmanuel Church, Alexandria, Louisiana (1937-41); First Baptist Church, Longview, Texas (1941-45); and Temple Baptist Church, Los Angeles, California (1945-50). In 1945, Howard Payne College awarded him an honorary doctor’s degree. He was for many years a Baptist youth leader, and in 1950, was elected secretary of the Student Department of the Baptist Sunday School Board, Nashville, Tennessee. He also served seven years on the Executive Committee of the Southern Baptist Convention, and on the Board of Trustees of East Texas Baptist College.

Teach Me, O LORD, Your Way of Truth

Hymn Story/Background

Psalm 119 is an acrostic psalm with twenty-two eight-verse segments, each beginning with a subsequent
letter in the Hebrew alphabet. The psalm focuses on the decrees, laws, commands, and promises of God. It depicts life as a walk or journey down a path; it lifts up the importance of a righteous heart, mouth, and voice, and righteous feet; it challenges us to treasure and take delight; it presents God’s law as both command and promise; and it gives us hope. The carefully constructed structure of the psalm fits the message: God’s Torah represents the establishment of order and poise in the middle of chaos and strife. Note that within the psalm’s precise and highly regulated structure there are many references to chaos and strife—including references to enemies, the arrogant, persecutors, and the psalmist’s own “wandering like a lost sheep” (v. 176). 
— Psalms for All Seasons (http://www.psalmsforallseasons.org)
The text comes from three long-meter settings for Psalm 119 in the 1912 Psalter. That psalter included a separate musical setting for each of Psalm 119's twenty-two biblical stanzas. 
— Bert Polman

Author Information

The 1912 Psalter was the first ecumenical psalter published in the United States and the most widely used metrical psalter of the twentieth century in North America.  The United Presbyterian Church invited all other Reformed and Presbyterian denominations to join them in the effort to provide a new versifications of the psalms; six Presbyterian denominations, as well as the Christian Reformed Church and the Reformed Church in America joined in the effort in revising the 1887 Psalter (whose texts actually dated back to the 1871 Book of Psalms; the 1887 edition had added music to the texts.).  The 1912 Psalter included all the psalms in 413 settings, eight doxologies, and the three Lukan canticles (Song of Mary, Song of Zechariah, and Song of Simeon).
— Bert Polman and Jack Reiffer

Further Reflections on Confessions and Statements of Faith References

It is vitally important that worshipers understand the role of God’s law among us. God gives his law to us, not so that we can earn his favor by full obedience, for even those converted to God cannot obey this law perfectly. Heidelberg Catechism, Lord’s Day 44, Question and Answer 114 says, “In this life even the holiest have only a small beginning of this obedience.” Instead, says Heidelberg Catechism, Lord’s Day 2, Question and Answer 3, through this law “we come to know [our] misery.” 

 

Yet in their new life of gratitude, God’s children “with all seriousness of purpose, do begin to live according to all, not only some, of God’s commandments” (Heidelberg Catechism, Lord’s Day 44, Question and Answer 114). They measure their good works of gratitude as “those which are done out of true faith, conform to God’s law, and are done for God’s glory” (Heidelberg Catechism, Lord’s Day 33, Question and Answer 91). 

 

In other words, though Christ has fulfilled the law for us, “The truth and substance of these things remain for us in Jesus Christ…[and] we continue to use the witnesses drawn from the law and prophets to confirm us in the gospel and to regulate our lives with full integrity for the glory of God according to the will of God” (Belgic Confession, Article 25). Therefore, the Ten Commandments with explanation are included in the third section, “gratitude,” (Lord’s Days 34-44) of Heidelberg Catechism.


Teach Me O Lord I Pray

Teach Me O Lord I Pray was written in 1959 by Gilbert Kearnie Keegan. Keegan grew up in Louisiana, graduated seminary from Southwestern in 1933, and pastored several churches, including First Baptist Longview, TX. He also was on the board of trustees for East Texas Baptist University. Shortly after writing this hymn, Keegan died of a heart attack in 1960.

Sir George Job Elvey wrote this tune called “Diademata” in 1868. While Matthew Bridges had written Crown Him With Many Crowns in 1851, later supplemented by Godfrey Thring, Elvey placed Bridge’s lyrics with his new melody. Even though Diademata is closely associated with Crown Him With Many Crowns, Charles Wesley’s Soldiers of Christ, Arise occupies this tune as well.

Keegan’s main passage for writing Teach Me O Lord I Pray was Psalm 143:10, which says, “Teach me to do your will, for you are my God. May your gracious Spirit lead me on level ground.” The surrounding lyrics help us in our request for God, through His Spirit, to continually make us more like Himself. 

The first verse starts off by saying, 

Teach me O Lord I pray. Your precious truth divine. Lead me to understand Your Word, and make its precepts mine. Impart Your wisdom Lord. Shed light upon my way, that I may know Your boundless love; Teach me O Lord I pray

The first thing that can be noticed about this particular song is its constant reference to prayer. All three verses start out with asking God to do something and mention that the person asking is praying and requesting. We often overlook prayer as an inefficient use of our time. In light of God’s mighty sovereignty, we see prayer as useless because we can’t use it to change anything around us. Surely this is a miserable misunderstanding of the purpose of praying.

In Matthew 6:5-18 when Jesus is teaching on how prayer and fasting should be accomplished, we see that the purpose of prayer is not to use God to get what we want in the world but instead to conform to God’s will in spite of what we may want. When we pray, we should be asking for God’s will to be done in our lives, meaning that we would live and work for God’s glory. In this sense, prayer is purposed to transform the believer, not necessarily change the situation.

When we sing this song, we are asking God, through the Holy Spirit, to help us conform our lives to God’s Word, specifically His “precious truth divine.”

Paul writes in 1 Corinthians 1:18-25 about the wisdom of God and its ability to confound those who have human wisdom. To mankind, the cross and resurrection makes no sense because it’s shameful for a man to die on a cross and impossible for a dead man to rise from the grave. But we know that these things are true, and so God’s wisdom is “a stumbling block to the Jews and foolishness to the Gentiles.” It’s by His grace that the truth of His Wisdom and death and resurrection has been revealed to us, through the working of the Holy Spirit.

Isaiah 11:2 tells us that the Spirit of the Lord is a “Spirit of wisdom and of understanding, [a] Spirit of counsel and of might, [a] Spirit of the knowledge and fear of the LORD.” The Spirit indwells or lives inside of the believer to act as a down payment of eternal security and also to encourage and convict us according to God’s Word.

Paul writes to the church in Ephesus and tells them “I keep asking that the God of our Lord Jesus Christ, the glorious Father, may give you the Spirit of wisdom and revelation, so that you may know him better” (Ephesians 1:17).

When we read God’s Word, the Spirit enables us to understand and produces repentance and a desire to do what God’s Word says. As much as we read, we should be praying and vice versa.

There are many passages from Psalm 119 that could be used in conjunction with this song, but for this verse, Psalm 119:105 is perfectly fitting. “Your word is a lamp for my feet and a light on my path.” God’s Word is able to guide us in how we should live our lives and it’s the Holy Spirit that reminds and encourages us to do so.

The second verse goes on to say,

Train me O Lord I pray, in knowledge and in skill. O daily let me walk with You, and do Your perfect will. O train my heart and mind to serve You unashamed, at home, abroad, where’er the Christ has yet to be proclaimed.

As we learn and understand God’s Word through prayer and the work of the Holy Spirit, we are left to obey God’s Word. James writes to Jewish believers that, “it is sin to know the good and yet not do it” (James 4:17). The commands and precepts taught to us should be followed and fulfilled with obedience. In 1st Samuel 15, King Saul offers up sacrifices to God in direct disobedience to God’s prophet Samuel. Samuel rebukes him by saying, “Does the Lord take pleasure in burnt offerings and sacrifices as much as in obeying the Lord? Look: to obey is better than sacrifice” (1 Samuel 15:22b).

Paul writes in Romans 12:1-2, “Therefore, brothers and sisters, in view of the mercies of God, I urge you to present your bodies as a living sacrifice, holy and pleasing to God; this is your true worship. Do not be conformed to this age, but be transformed by the renewing of your mind, so that you may discern what is the good, pleasing, and perfect will of God.”

Paul goes on to say, “serve the Lord” (Romans 12:11), because service is a natural response that shows thankfulness for what God has done for us in Christ Jesus. Christ’s death and resurrection has freed us from sin and death and saved us to be slaves to righteousness. This demands us to be “living sacrifices…holy and pleasing to God.”

The progression from life change to worship continues with evangelism. Jesus commands His disciples after His resurrection, “Go, therefore, and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, teaching them to observe everything I have commanded you. And remember, I am with you always, to the end of the age” (Matthew 28:19-20).

When We Walk With the Lord contains the prayer, “What You say we will do. Where You send we will go.” This has to be our resolve in relation to God’s Word and mission. I love that Keegan includes three areas: “At home, abroad, or where’er the Christ has yet to be proclaimed.” Being obedient in sharing the Gospel and making disciples doesn’t have to be done across the ocean. You can obey God’s command by loving your literal next-door neighbor and sharing the good news of Christ’s death and resurrection. 

The third and final verse concludes by saying,

Use me O Lord I pray to shed Your Light sublime. To rich and poor to high and low of ev’ry race and clime. O may Your flaming zeal burn constantly and true, till souls in darkness ev’rywhere fall down and worship You.

It’s similar to the last line of the second verse in its resolve to spread the good news of Christ to the nations. As we watch the idea of the initial prayer request in each verse, it’s interesting to note that while we pray for God to remove troubles and awkward situations, we’re using this song to ask God to put us in troubling and awkward situations.

For First Baptist Bonham, I pray that we would sing this song with a genuine desire to understand God’s truth and to obey God’s Word through prayer and the work of the Holy Spirit.
